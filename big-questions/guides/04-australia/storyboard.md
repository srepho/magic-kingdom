# Guide 04 — Who was here first? The story of Australia

**Built** 14 November 2026 without a review stop. The brief asks for extra care here, so the
choices that most need Stephen's eye are listed at the end. Fact checks are in `notes.md`.

**The local Country: Darug.** Stephen supplied where the family lives on 14 November 2026, and the
local council's own Acknowledgement names the Darug people as Traditional Custodians. It is named
in the try-it activity and the grown-up notes only, not on the map or in any caption. The suburb
and council are not named anywhere in the published files.

## The guide in one line

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Single idea | Many First peoples have lived here for an immense time and still do. British ships are very recent |
| Her action | She slides along a very long strip of time, then stretches its last sliver |
| What she sees | A map that is a patchwork of many Countries the whole way; a hair-thin red line at the very end; and, stretched out, what happened inside that line |
| Misconception to avoid | That the story starts with ships. That there was one people. That 1788 was the first contact. That the First peoples are in the past |

**Taught terms (two):** **First peoples**, **Country**.

## Opening — footprints

| Field | |
| --- | --- |
| The insight | People walked here long before you |
| What she does | Drags a finger across the sand; taps the water |
| What she sees | Her own footprints follow her finger. A wave washes them away and uncovers older footprints set hard in the ground; each wave uncovers more, fainter and more crowded |
| What she can vary | Where she walks; when to call the wave; up to three older layers |
| Misconception to avoid | That she, or her family, is the first to be here |
| How we know it works | She walks again after a wave and looks at the old prints beside hers |

Captions: *"The wave washed yours away. But look: older footprints!"* (9) ·
*"People walked here long, long before you."* (7) ·
*"For a very, very long time. Longer than anyone can count."* (11)

## Centrepiece — two scales of time over a map

| Field | |
| --- | --- |
| The insight | The First peoples' time here is almost the whole strip. Everything since the British ships is a hair-thin line at the end |
| What she does | Slides a handle along the strip. Taps the thin red line, or the stretch button, to stretch that line across the strip; taps again to let it go. In the long view, taps the map |
| What she sees | Long view: the patchwork map, steady, with the sea lower in the ice age; a tapped Country glows. Close view: a Dutch ship calls in the north and leaves; boats from Makassar come and go each year; British ships arrive at Sydney and stay; towns appear and the patchwork pales outward from them; near today some colour returns |
| What she can vary | Where to stop; back and forth; in and out of the close view as often as she likes |
| Misconception to avoid | See the table below |
| How we know it works | She goes back to the long view to find the red line again |

How each brief rule is met:

| Rule | How |
| --- | --- |
| Begin with the First peoples, not ships | The guide opens on footprints, and the strip starts 65,000 years back. No ship appears until she stretches the last sliver. A test checks that the first four captions mention no ships |
| Present tense; living cultures | The last caption is "are still here… are alive and strong". The patchwork never loses all its colour, and a test checks that |
| Many peoples | About 290 tiles of many colours, plus the Torres Strait Islands. The caption says "many peoples… its own Country and its own language" |
| Local Country only from Stephen | Darug, from the council for the place Stephen gave. Named in try-it and the notes |
| 1788 is not first contact | The 1606 Dutch landing and the Makassan boats (from 1700) both come before the British ships, in that order, and a test checks it |
| The hard sentence, calmly | The brief's own words, 15 words: "The newcomers took land that was already someone's home. That hurt the people living there." |
| Plainly, not a toy | After 1788 nothing responds to a tap, nothing hops and there are no sounds. Only the time handle works |
| Not one even timeline | Two scales joined by a visible stretch. In the long view everything since 1788 is under half a percent of the strip |
| No Dreaming stories; no imitated art | None. The map is a plain modern patchwork of regular hexagons; dots were avoided so it could not read as dot painting |

Captions, long view: *"Long, long ago, people already lived all over this land."* (10) ·
*"Many peoples. Each has its own **Country** and its own language."* (10) ·
*"Their families have looked after their Country all this time."* (10) ·
*"They are Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples: the **First peoples**."* (11) ·
*"See the thin red line? Ships came then. Tap it to look closer."* (13)

Captions, close view: *"This is the thin red line, stretched out. Slide on."* (10) ·
*"A ship from far away stopped in the north, then sailed off."* (12) ·
*"Boats from Makassar came each year to fish and trade, then went home."* (13) ·

*"The newcomers took land that was already someone's home. That hurt the people living there."* (15) ·
*"The First peoples never left. They have won some of their land back."* (13) ·
*"The **First peoples** are still here. Their cultures are alive and strong."* (12)

## Decisions that most need a second look

1. **The patchwork of made-up hexagons** for "many Countries". The notes say it is not real boundaries and link to the AIATSIS map.
2. **Paling colour** as the one picture for land taken and harm done, and **colour returning** for land rights and cultural renewal. Which tiles regain colour is invented.
3. **Colonisation drawn as even rings from seven towns**, reaching most of the continent by about 1900.
4. **65,000 years** as the start of the strip, the older estimate, with the dispute in the notes.
5. **"Boats from Makassar… to fish and trade"**, starting at 1700 (the National Museum's "at least 1700").
6. **The caption "They have won some of their land back"** for the years after 1970.
7. **No words from First languages are printed.** Only one greeting could be checked, so the guide points to recordings by speakers instead.
8. **Two books by First Nations authors** are named; no place to visit is named.
9. **The beach scene is a metaphor.** The real 20,000-year-old footprints were uncovered by wind.
10. **The answer to "Is it my fault?"** in the notes is unlabelled, as it is not a fact claim.
