Try it: leader for the afternoon

  1. Put out two cups. Draw a picture on each for two people in the family.
  2. Everyone drops one block in the cup of the person they choose. That is your vote.
  3. Tip the cups out. Whoever has the most blocks is the leader for the afternoon.
  4. Agree together which choices the leader may make. The leader listens, then chooses one game.
  5. Now try a council of three toys. Give each one a choice of game. Which game gets more votes?

You need: two cups, a few blocks or pegs, paper and a pencil. It works best with three or more people, or line up some toys to vote too.

Listen before deciding

Give three toys different ideas for a game. Let each have a turn to “speak”. Can you find a plan they can share? A vote is one way to decide, and talking or taking turns can help too.

Wonder more

  • If you were the leader, what is the first thing you would decide?
  • What could you do if the leader decided something you didn't like?
  • The most votes wins. How do you think the others feel?

Play another way

Pick one invitation, then let your child lead. Watching, pointing or changing her mind all count; there is no answer to pass.

  • Keep everyone’s preferences the same and vote twice in the town. Then change one voter’s mind and vote again.
  • Open Decide together. Keep the leader’s hill choice and give the other two river choices. Count their votes.
  • Ask: “What could the group do for people who wanted the other plan?” Accept more than one idea.

The idea

  • FactAustralia is a representative democracy. Citizens aged 18 and over vote to choose people, called representatives, who then make decisions on everyone's behalf. People do not vote on each rule.
  • FactThere are elections again every few years, so voters can keep their representatives or choose new ones.
  • FactAustralia has three levels of government. Local councils look after things like rubbish collection and parks. States and territories look after things like schools and hospitals. The federal government looks after things like defence, immigration and passports.
  • FactThere are more than 500 local councils across Australia.
  • FactVoting in federal elections is compulsory for enrolled citizens aged 18 and over.

What the pictures simplify

  • ModelOne leader in the town. This first toy isolates choosing someone and choosing again. It is not how a council or parliament makes every decision. The "Decide together" scene adds other elected voices: even the leader can be outvoted. Leaders must follow the law.
  • ModelThree members in "Decide together". This is an invented council, not a model of every council rule. Each member gets one vote between two plans. Actual federal laws need agreement in both houses of Parliament and assent. Debate, amendments, rights and legal limits matter too.
  • ModelTwo candidates and "most votes wins". Real elections usually have several candidates, and Australian voters number them in order of preference.
  • ModelOpen voting. In the toy you can see how everyone votes. Real votes are secret, so nobody can be pressured.
  • ModelOne thing to disagree about. The townsfolk care only which side of town gets built on. Real voters weigh many things at once.
  • ModelNo parties. Left out on purpose. The guide shows how choosing works, not who should win.
  • ModelHands up. The opening is a direct vote among friends, which works for small groups. The town shows why bigger groups choose people instead.
  • ModelThe map. A rough drawn outline. It leaves out the territories and the other states' borders.

Questions she may ask

  • Who is in charge of Australia? FactThe people we elect to Parliament make the laws. The leader of the government is called the Prime Minister. The King is the head of state, and the Governor-General does that job for him here, but neither runs the country day to day.
  • Can children vote? FactNot until they are 18. Children can still say what they think, and grown-ups can listen.
  • What if a leader does something bad? FactThe rules apply to leaders too, and judges can say no. And at the next election people can choose someone else.
  • Who do you vote for? ModelA neutral answer: "Voting is private, and people in the same family or street often choose differently. When you are grown up you will make up your own mind."
  • Is it fair that the most votes wins? UnsurePeople have argued about this for a very long time. Most agree it is fairer than one person deciding for ever, and that the winners should still look after everyone.

Words this guide teaches

Vote: showing which one you choose. Leader: someone who helps a group make decisions. Government: the people who run public services and help make rules for a place.
